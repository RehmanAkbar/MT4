# The Measured Move Strategy

Strategy extracted from a trading video transcript. The presenter's claim: this single
concept is what turned around six years of losing performance and has carried 17 years
of profitable trading since. No indicators, no market theory — just addition and
subtraction applied to the size of price swings.

---

## 1. Core Premise

Markets do not "go up" or "go down." They go **up, then down, then up, then down** — a
rhythmic sequence. Every instrument (index, stock, FX, crypto, oil) has its own fairly
reliable rhythm, and that rhythm repeats daily on every asset in every market.

A **measured move** is simply the size of one leg of that rhythm, expressed in the units
you trade: points, pips, or dollars per share.

Two numbers describe the rhythm:

| Leg | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Impulse leg** | The directional thrust | $3.00 |
| **Pullback leg** | The counter-move that follows | $1.00 |

Once you have those two numbers, you have a formula:

```
+3  →  -1  →  +3  →  -1  →  +3  ...
```

And critically, the formula **flips with the direction**. If the up-move fails, the
probability instantly shifts to the mirror sequence:

```
-3  →  +1  →  -3  →  +1  →  flat  →  +3 ...
```

The open-to-close daily bar looks like one clean green candle. Intraday it is never
clean — price "takes the scenic route" from A to B. Each of those detours is a measured
move, and each is predictable in **size**, which tells you three things:

1. How far this leg should run (your target).
2. How deep the following pullback should be (your entry zone and your stop distance).
3. Whether the move is still healthy (see §5, the momentum read).

This is what flag patterns are actually exploiting underneath the surface — you buy the
base of the flag because that base *is* the measured pullback, and you target the pole's
distance because that pole *is* the measured impulse.

## 2. The Top-Down Workflow

Run these three steps in order, every day, before taking a trade.

### Step 1 — Establish the daily range

Start every session by measuring the daily range over the **last one to two weeks**.
Literally count it off the daily bars: high 20,700 → low 20,400 = a 300-point day. Scan
back and find the number that most days respect.

Shortcut: put **ATR(14) on the daily chart**. In the worked example it read 313 → call it
300. You do not need precision, you need a benchmark to work back from.

This sets your expectations for the entire day:

- **Do not ask the market for 750 points when it delivers 300.** That expectation is
  what makes you hold winners into reversals.
- **If price is already +320 off the open with no news, the first move is not a buy.**
  All the meat has been stripped off the bone. You need the measured pullback to get a
  risk/reward worth taking.
- **If price is -350 into the low of the range, do not initiate shorts there.** Millions
  of range-bound traders are working those exact levels, which is why you see so much
  activity around them.

Adjust the benchmark for regime. In the recorded session (post-election, pre-FOMC), the
daily candles had been visibly expanding — so 400/500/600 points was live even though
the ATR said 300. Volatility regime overrides the raw average.

### Step 2 — Measure the intraday legs

Drop to the **5-minute chart** and measure the last several days of swings. In the
example the legs read:

- Impulse legs: roughly **200–250 points**
- Pullback legs: roughly **100–150 points**
- Sequence: 200 up / 100 down / 200 up / 100 down — "not to the penny, but pretty close"

Now the rules write themselves. Buying at the start of a fresh leg and asking for 150–200
points is not asking too much. Buying after 200 points has already printed and asking for
another 250 as a day trade is insane.

> "It's not the idea, it's the execution."

### Step 3 — Time the entry off the pullback, never the breakout

This is the single highest-value takeaway:

**You cannot buy the top-heavy measured move in succession, especially into resistance.**

- The **newer trader buys the break**. By then a big chunk of the measured move is
  already consumed, so the target is unreachable and the stop is far — "you're just
  cutting your own throat."
- The **experienced trader executes in the base**, where the measured pullback has
  completed and ideally lines up with other context.

Waiting for the pull is what gives you options. Buying the extension only gives you the
chance to discover you were wrong at the worst price.

## 3. Required Context (Confluence)

The presenter is explicit that the measured move alone is not the whole edge — "the more
context you have, the better your results." His early technical analysis failed because
he traded flags and breakouts with no context. Stack these on top of the measurement:

- **Support / resistance and pivots.** A pullback that terminates *at* a level is worth
  far more than one that terminates in open air.
- **Position within the daily range.** Top of range = don't chase longs. Bottom of range
  = don't chase shorts.
- **Break-and-retest.** A retest that arrives at the same distance as the typical
  measured pullback is a high-quality entry.
- **Swing structure.** A chain of higher lows followed by higher highs tells you the
  market is buying dips — which is direct permission to keep buying measured pullbacks.
- **Reversal candle signal.** Engulfing candles, hammers / inverted hammers, tower
  reversals. The measured move tells you *where* the turn should happen; the candle tells
  you *that it is happening*. You still need the market to show you it is ready to be
  bought.
- **News / event regime.** Elections, FOMC. Expands ranges and makes moves impulsive.

Note the natural tension the presenter accepts: markets frequently open in the middle of
the range or directly at resistance — an awkward spot by design. "Don't diddle in the
middle." The measured move is precisely the tool for navigating those awkward opens.

## 4. Trade Construction

### Entry

Enter at the completion of a measured pullback, confirmed by context (§3), in the
direction of the prevailing leg sequence.

### Stop

Place the stop beyond the **full measured pullback / backtest distance**, not tighter.

Worked example: the prior pullback ran 20,720 → 20,620 = 100 points. Entry was 20,682.
So the stop goes ~100 points down, below 20,620 (or below the engulfing candle's low).

Position-sizing rule that follows directly:

> **"If you can't give up that much, you can't take this trade."**

The stop is defined by the market's rhythm, not by your comfort. If the required stop is
larger than your risk budget, you size down or you skip it. You do not tighten the stop
into the noise band.

### Target

The target is the measured impulse leg. In the example, 100–150 points from the 20,682
entry → roughly 20,782 / 20,832.

Take the impulsive early move even if it comes in slightly short of the full projection.
"You're never going to get the tops and bottoms."

### Re-entry

Do **not** re-enter in the same direction until a full measured pullback has been shaved
off — roughly 100 points in the example — ideally as a break-and-retest that matches the
measured pullback distance, back into support. Then buy that dip for the next impulse.

Expect the pullback even on a day that has ramped from the open. And if the rare day
comes that never gives it to you, that is fine — **you stick with the formula anyway**
(see §6).

## 5. The Momentum Read — Diagnosing Move Health

The depth of each pullback relative to the *typical* measured pullback is a live read on
who is in control:

- **Pullbacks arriving shallower than the typical measured distance** → aggressive
  demand, buyers are front-running the level, continuation is likely. In the example
  price "wasn't even getting down into the full distance of the typical measured move,"
  and each dip was bought and rocketed.
- **A down-leg with no momentum in it is not a short.** If the counter-move can't
  generate thrust, it is a pullback, not a reversal.
- **A leg visibly running out of momentum won't give you much more.** Common sense:
  if thrust is already fading, don't expect the rest of the projection.

That read is also the "secret" the presenter refers to: as long as the market keeps
buying the measured pullback *or less*, it is telling you it wants to be bought.

## 6. Applying It to Reversals / Down Moves

The method is symmetric. While a market is dumping and everyone is panicking:

1. Calculate the measured move down.
2. Locate the supports further down the ladder.
3. Project: "this probably goes 20–40 points further, finds a buyer in there, and
   bounces 30–40 points back up."
4. Wait for the reversal signal (hammer, engulfing, tower) to confirm the turn.
5. Trade *that piece* — you are not trying to call the ultimate bottom.

If you buy into a base with a tight stop and the base fails, you already know the next
stop is the lower level — you exit small and try again there. That is how you avoid
"losing money in droves on your bad trades."

---

## 7. Trading Philosophy Behind the Method

- **Judge by the sample, not the trade.** "If I did this 100 times, what would my overall
  result be?" Chasing an extended move might work once in a bull market on a unique
  circumstance — by trade 10 you'd be bankrupt. What you do once doesn't count.
- **Trade with a purpose,** not "trading to trade." Every entry should have a stated
  expected move and a stated invalidation before it's placed.
- **Set honest expectations.** Most intraday panic — bailing on two red candles, FOMO-ing
  into two green ones — comes from not knowing what the normal rhythm is.
- **Emotions are the abandonment of intellect.** In euphoric or panicked conditions the
  rhythm expands, so measure the new rhythm rather than assuming the old one or assuming
  the move can't continue. "You can never say never in the market."
- **There are no guarantees.** A projected 150-point leg may run 20 points and dump.
  The point is not certainty; it is being positioned in the right spot, with a defined
  risk, often enough that the good spots pay for the bad ones.

## 8. Quantified Rule Set

The strategy reduced to mechanical form, for backtesting or coding.

**Daily preparation**

| Variable | Definition |
|---|---|
| `DailyRange` | ATR(14) on D1, or manual count of the last 5–10 daily bars |
| `RegimeMultiplier` | >1 when daily candles are visibly expanding (news/event regime) |
| `ExpectedDayRange` | `DailyRange * RegimeMultiplier` |

**Intraday measurement (M5)**

| Variable | Definition |
|---|---|
| `ImpulseLeg` | Median size of recent same-direction thrust legs |
| `PullbackLeg` | Median size of recent counter-moves between those thrusts |
| `PullbackRatio` | `PullbackLeg / ImpulseLeg` (≈0.33–0.5 in the examples) |

**Entry filter — all must be true**

1. Current move off the session open is **less than** `ExpectedDayRange` (room left).
2. Price has retraced **at least ~1×** `PullbackLeg` from the last swing extreme.
3. The pullback terminates at a level: support/resistance, pivot, or breakout-retest.
4. Swing structure agrees (higher lows for longs, lower highs for shorts).
5. A reversal candle prints at the level (engulfing / hammer / tower).
6. Price is **not** at the opposite extreme of the daily range from your direction.

**Order parameters**

| Parameter | Rule |
|---|---|
| Entry | At close of the confirming reversal candle at the pullback terminus |
| Stop | Beyond the full `PullbackLeg` distance from entry / beyond the signal candle |
| Target | `1 × ImpulseLeg` from entry (banked early on impulsive extension) |
| Sizing | Derived from the stop. If risk > budget, reduce size or skip — never tighten |
| Re-entry lockout | No new same-direction entry until price retraces ≥ `1 × PullbackLeg` |

**Invalidation / regime flip**

If the impulse fails and price breaks the pullback origin, the sequence inverts: expect
`1 × ImpulseLeg` in the *opposite* direction, with `PullbackLeg` counter-bounces.

## 9. Worked Example from the Transcript

Index futures, day after the US election, FOMC two days out.

| Step | Reading |
|---|---|
| Daily ATR(14) | 313 → benchmark 300 points, but regime expanding → 400–600 plausible |
| M5 impulse legs | ~200–250 points |
| M5 pullback legs | ~100–150 points |
| Pre-open state | +330 on the day, pressing into resistance |
| Last up leg | 20,580 → 20,720 ≈ 130 points, top-heavy, into resistance → **not buyable** |
| Setup taken | Pullback 20,720 → ~20,660 (≈60 pts) into a pivot, engulfing candle, higher-low structure |
| Entry | **Long 20,682** |
| Stop | ~100 points, below 20,620 (the full prior pullback) |
| Target | 100–150 points → 20,782 / 20,832 |
| Outcome | Sold into the impulsive open drive near 20,800 |
| Follow-up | Waited for a ~100-point retrace back into M5 support → another 100+ point bounce |

On a normal (non-event) day, the presenter's default at that pre-open location would have
been the opposite trade: **short into resistance at the top of the daily range**, which
is the same rule applied in mirror.

## 10. One-Paragraph Summary

Measure the daily range to know how much the instrument gives you in a session. Measure
the intraday impulse and pullback legs to learn its rhythm. Never enter on the extension —
wait for a pullback of roughly the measured pullback size, arriving at a level, with
structure and a reversal candle agreeing. Risk the full measured pullback distance and
size accordingly; if that stop is too big for you, pass on the trade. Target one measured
impulse leg and bank it. Lock out re-entry until another full pullback prints. If the
impulse fails, flip the formula and expect the same distances in the other direction.
Judge the method over a hundred trades, not over the one in front of you.
